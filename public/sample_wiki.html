The Method of Loci.

On the ancient art of remembering by walking — and on the literal floors, rooms, and pinned objects with which Mnemon reproduces it for software.

Compiled from 11 verbatim sources Backlinks 17 Locus palace / east wing / I Last sealed 2026-04-26

The earliest treatises on memory describe a curious habit: a man who wished to remember a list of things would build a building inside his head, room by room, and place the things along the walls. To recall them, he would walk the rooms again. The technique survived antiquity, the Renaissance, and the rise of paper, and now it survives the rise of software — in Mnemon, the building is real, the rooms are tables, and the walking is a query.

The technique is called the method of loci, sometimes the art of memory, and in the older Latin sources memoria artificiosa1. Cicero attributes its discovery to the poet Simonides of Ceos, who, having left a banquet shortly before its roof collapsed onto the assembled guests, was able to identify each crushed body by recalling where the man had been sitting. The story is grim and apocryphal in equal measure, but it contains the technique's essential insight: place is a stronger handle on memory than meaning.

A trained mind walks its own rooms the way a librarian walks the stacks — knowing not what is on each shelf, but knowing exactly where to look. Field manual, Vol. I, §1

Modern retrieval systems have, on the whole, abandoned this insight. They chunk a document into 800-token windows, pass each window through an embedder, and dump the resulting vectors into an index that has forgotten the document's shape entirely. The chunks are findable but they are not placed. An agent that retrieves them has a list of fragments; what it does not have is a building.

§ 1.01.II The two operations: sealing & placing

Mnemon performs two distinct operations on every memory it is given. The first is sealing: the verbatim source — a chat transcript, a file, a screenshot, a webhook payload — is hashed, written once, and never modified.2 The second is placing: a reference to the sealed source is pinned at a locus inside a room, where it can be recalled by name, by neighbour, or by query.

Sealing without placing is a backup. Placing without sealing is a hallucination waiting to happen. The combination is what allows an agent to follow any summary or wiki entry back to the byte-perfect record that authored it — and what allows you, the operator, to audit what the agent has actually been told.

Vocabulary

  • Verbatim
    The sealed, content-addressed original. A row in mnemon_verbatim. Never edited; only superseded.
  • Entry
    A compiled summary that cites one or more verbatims. The unit you read in the wiki.
  • Locus
    The pinned position of an entry inside a room. Coordinates are real; the palace is the floor plan.
  • Room
    A coherent context — a project, a client, a research thread. Rooms have walls; queries respect them.
  • Walk
    An ordered traversal of loci. Used by agents to reconstruct context without flooding the model.

§ 1.01.III Why this matters for agents

A modern agent runs in a context window of a few hundred thousand tokens. That sounds like a great deal of room until you recall that an active project may produce that many tokens in a single afternoon. The agent that wants to be useful tomorrow morning must know how to not remember everything at once — to stand at the doorway of a room and select what to bring inside.

Mnemon gives that decision a vocabulary. Rather than asking what is relevant?, the agent asks which room am I in?, what loci are within reach?, and which verbatim sources do those loci cite?. Each question narrows the candidate set by an order of magnitude, and each answer is auditable on the way out.

Note what the locus does not contain. There is no extracted summary, no embedding, no derived metadata. Those things exist — they are computed by the compile pipeline and live in their own tables — but they are downstream of the locus, never substituted for it. If the embedding model changes tomorrow, the locus does not.

Practical consequence

When a senior engineer asks you "what did the agent actually see when it made that decision?", you can answer them. You can show the verbatim, the locus it was placed at, the room it was placed in, and the query that pulled it out. You will rarely have this experience with a retrieval-augmented system that has been built without sealing in mind.

The remainder of this volume is concerned with the apparatus that makes this possible: a single Postgres database, a small Laravel package, and a handful of MCP tools that turn the database into a building your agents can walk.
